This page is a short manual for working with me. It is what I would tell a new manager or teammate in our first week, so we can skip a few months of finding out.

## What you can count on me for

- **I know where things stand.** If you ask me how a project is doing, I can tell you what is done, what is late, and why. I keep a simple tracker and I keep it up to date.
- **You hear about problems early.** I would rather tell you about a risk that never happens than surprise you with a problem that did. When I raise something, I also bring a proposal.
- **I write things down.** Decisions, instructions, how a process works. The goal is that the next person does not have to start from zero, and that the work continues when I am on holiday.
- **I finish things.** The last 10% of a project, the go-live, the training, the documentation, is where many projects lose energy. I like that part.

## How I run a project

I start by agreeing on what "done" means, who decides, and who needs to be kept informed. Then I make the work visible: usually one tracker with owners and dates, reviewed every week. <!-- TODO(Vincent): true? -->

I prefer short, regular check-ins over long status meetings. A good status update fits on one screen: what changed, what is at risk, and what I need from whom.

When a plan comes from far away, such as headquarters or a partner in another time zone, I translate it into what it means locally, and I check early whether the people who have to do the work agree with it.

## How I communicate

- **Direct and friendly.** Living in the Netherlands for nine years has made me more direct. Growing up in China means I also notice what is not being said.
- **In writing first, then a call.** I like to send a short written summary before a meeting, so the meeting is for deciding, not for reading.
- **In three languages.** I work in English, speak Dutch at B2 level (NT2 Staatsexamen II), and Mandarin is my native language. With Chinese suppliers or colleagues I can switch to Mandarin when it helps, and I can explain what a message really means to both sides.

## Working between Chinese and Dutch teams

I have spent most of my career in between: Chinese companies in Europe (Huawei, Tencent), and American and Dutch companies working with Chinese partners.

What I have learned is that most misunderstandings are not about language. They are about expectations. A Dutch team expects to hear "no" when something is not possible. A Chinese team may say "we will try" and mean the same thing. Headquarters may expect a quick "yes"; a local team may need time to agree first. ## When things go wrong

I stay calm and start with facts: what happened, who is affected, and what we do in the next hour. The discussion about why it happened comes after the fix, and it is about the process, not about blaming a person.

## What I am still working on

- **Saying no sooner.** I like helping people, and sometimes I take on too much. I now write down what I agreed to, so it is easier to see when my plate is full.

## What gives me energy

Something real at the end of the work. A customer who is up and running, a supplier who delivered on time, a tool that people actually use on Monday morning. And a team that talks openly, including about what is not going well.
