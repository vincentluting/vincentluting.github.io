In 2017, Maimai, a Chinese career app a bit like LinkedIn, asked more than ten thousand office workers a simple question: do you feel lonely?

61% said yes. One in five said every day.

Then they asked a second question, and this is the one I keep thinking about. How much money do you spend on it?

Most people had an answer. The biggest group said 1,000 to 3,000 yuan a month. The second biggest group said 3,000 to 5,000. For a young office worker in Beijing in 2017, that is a lot of money. For many of them it was a big part of what was left after rent.

The report is called 孤独经济, "the loneliness economy". It is 56 pages long, and half of it is not really about people. It is about business: takeaway food, live streaming, online games, mini karaoke boxes, smart speakers, shops without staff. Who makes money from lonely people, and how much.

I read it again recently. Two things surprised me. The first is how much of it is about me. The second is that it mixes up two different things, and I only noticed because I spend my days moving between three languages.

## I was in the 72%

72% of the people in the survey lived away from their hometown. The people in the report's stories come from Heilongjiang, Jilin and Anhui, and they all moved to Beijing for work.

I went the other way. In 2015 I left China for a master's in Dutch at Leiden. The plan was two years. In 2017, when this survey was done, those two years were ending and I was deciding to stay.

So I was not in the sample. But I made the same move, only to a different city.

The story that stayed with me is a small one. A woman the report calls Liu Ying had a high fever and went to the hospital alone. She sat there with a drip in her hand and looked at the other patients. She took out her phone to ask someone to come and sit with her. Then she put it away again, because she did not know who to call.

I think everyone who has moved far from home knows that moment, or is a little afraid of it. For me it is less a feeling than a very practical question. If something happens to me tonight, who comes?

## One word, two jobs

This is where my linguistics degree gets in the way of a good story.

The report defines the loneliness economy like this: some people do not like talking to others when they shop, eat or go out. They prefer to do these things alone, best of all without even speaking to a waiter. The businesses that serve them are the loneliness economy.

But that is not loneliness. That is being alone. Plenty of people who like being alone are not lonely at all.

English has *alone* and *lonely*. Dutch has *alleen* and *eenzaam*, and nobody mixes them up. "Ik ben alleen thuis" means you are home by yourself on a Tuesday evening. "Ik ben eenzaam" is something you only say to a good friend. Chinese has the difference too: 独处 is being on your own, often by choice, and 孤独 is the feeling. The report even uses 独处 on its last page, when it tells readers to enjoy their time alone. But for fifty pages before that, 孤独 does both jobs.

Once you separate the two words, the report splits into two lists.

The first list is products for being alone. Takeaway. Mini karaoke boxes in shopping malls, 30 yuan for 15 minutes. Convenience stores with no staff. Small apartments with small furniture. There is nothing wrong with these. I like eating alone sometimes. They just do not do anything about loneliness. One man in the report, a designer, said singing alone in a karaoke box made him feel lonelier than eating hotpot alone. I believe him.

The second list is products that sell a feeling of company. Live streaming, online games, dating apps, and a smart speaker that one company launched as a "cure for loneliness". These are closer to the real problem. And here the report is more honest than I expected. A man who tipped live streamers for months said that after a while it felt like paying someone to keep him company. In the chapter about AI, the writers say it directly: these companies make money from lonely people, and the people stay lonely.

## The company on page 30

On page 30 there is a short story about a man who logged into an old game from his university days. His friends were all gone, the maps had changed, and he walked around the empty game world and suddenly felt very lonely. Right after it come Tencent's numbers. In the third quarter of 2017, online games brought Tencent 26.8 billion yuan, 48% more than a year before.

Since 2023 I have worked at Tencent, in IT for three of its game studios in Europe. My days are about accounts, security tools and Perforce licences. Loneliness never came up.

I don't want to make games the bad guy here. Some of my best evenings were around a board game with friends, and a lot of people play online with friends they also know in real life. But the report made me look at a revenue number from the other side. Part of those 26.8 billion yuan came from people playing at two in the morning because there was nobody else to talk to. I think both things are true at the same time.

## What the report shows without saying it

If you skip the business chapters and only look at the people, the report says something quite simple.

The five most common reasons people gave for feeling lonely were living far from home, being single, not having enough money, not having friends, and problems at work. In a Beijing study the report quotes, 67.5% of young people working in the internet industry had five or fewer friends in the city they could really trust.

Then look at who in the report feels better. It is not the man in the karaoke box, and not the man tipping the streamer. It is Zhipeng, who left Beijing after thirteen years and moved back to Hefei, where his brother, his old classmates and his family live. It is the home-sharing hosts who say the best part is cooking and talking with their guests. It is the father who feels less alone since his daughter was born.

You cannot order any of these with an app. They are people, and the years it takes to really know them.

The report ends with advice: enjoy your time alone, read, think about your life. It is not bad advice. But it is advice for being alone. For being lonely, the answer is more boring and a lot harder. You have to stay in one place long enough for people to become your people.

## More than ten years later

I am still in the Netherlands. The two years became more than ten.

If the survey had asked me, I think my answer about money would have been close to zero. Not because I was never lonely, but because the things that helped never cost much. A train ticket to see a friend. A coffee that went on for two hours. Enough Dutch to understand the joke at a birthday party, and not just laugh one second after everyone else.

If I could add one question to that survey, it would be Liu Ying's question. Not "how lonely are you?" and not "how much do you spend?", but this one: if you were in hospital tonight, who would you call?

I think the answer would tell you more than all 56 pages. And unlike almost everything else in the report, nobody can sell it to you.
