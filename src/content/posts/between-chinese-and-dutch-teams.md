For most of my career, I have worked in the middle. At Huawei and Tencent, I worked for Chinese companies in Europe. At Amazon and Philips, I worked with partners and colleagues in many countries. I am Chinese, I speak Dutch, and I work in English every day.

People sometimes ask me what the biggest difference is between Chinese and Dutch teams. My answer is always the same: it is not the language. It is the expectations that nobody says out loud.

Here are five things I now do on every project that crosses these two worlds.

## 1. I ask what "yes" means

In a Dutch meeting, "yes" usually means yes, and "no" is said openly. In many Chinese teams, a direct "no" to a manager or partner is rude, so "we will try" or "it is a bit difficult" can mean no.

Neither is wrong. But if you don't know which one you are hearing, you will make a plan on something that was never agreed. So I ask a second, concrete question: "Can we confirm the date is the 15th?" A real yes survives that question. <!-- TODO(Vincent): a real example? -->

## 2. I find out who really decides

Dutch organisations are flat. The person in the meeting often decides. In many Chinese organisations, decisions are made higher up and later, and the person in the meeting has to check first.

When a Dutch team feels that "nothing is happening", it is often because the decision is still travelling. I try to find out early who needs to say yes, and I give them what they need to decide quickly.

## 3. I write it down, in both directions

After every important conversation, I send a short summary: what we agreed, who does what, and by when. It sounds boring. It is the single most useful habit I have. Written words travel across time zones and languages much better than a memory of a call.

## 4. I explain the "why", not only the "what"

Headquarters often sends instructions without context. Local teams then push back, because the instruction does not fit their market. Most of the time, the conflict disappears once both sides understand the reason. ## 5. I build trust before I need it

Trust between cultures is built one small problem at a time. I try to solve something small for a partner early, before there is a big problem. When the big problem comes, and it always does, we already know we can rely on each other.

## Why this matters to me now

I am looking for my next role in high-tech in the Netherlands, where many companies work with suppliers and customers in China. That is exactly the kind of middle I know well. If you work in that middle too, I would like to hear what you have learned.
