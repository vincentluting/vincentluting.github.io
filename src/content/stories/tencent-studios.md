## The situation

Tencent owns game studios around the world. Three of them in Europe were my clients. <!-- TODO(Vincent): can you name them, or should they stay anonymous? --> They are part of the same company, but they work like independent businesses. They have their own IT people, their own tools and their own opinions.

Central IT, where I worked, had tools and services that could help them: for version control, for security, for everyday IT. But "we have a tool for that" does not mean anyone will use it. A studio in the middle of making a game does not want to change tools because headquarters says so.

My job was to be the bridge. I was the studios' contact in central IT, and central IT's contact in the studios.

## The hard part

Trust. The studios had seen central initiatives come and go. If I showed up with a slide deck about a new tool, they would be polite and change nothing.

The second difficulty was that the product teams were far away, in Asia and the US, and in different time zones. When a studio needed a small change, or had a question the product team had to answer, it could take a long time. As EU Product Owner for Security and Foundation IT, I was also responsible for making sure European needs showed up in the product roadmaps at all.

## What I did

**I started by listening, not selling.** I met each studio to understand how they worked, what hurt, and what they were already paying for. <!-- TODO(Vincent): how did you do this? Regular syncs, interviews, a user journey map? There is a "Weekly Studio Support Sync" tracker in your Drive. --> Only then did I suggest anything.

**I matched tools to real problems.** Where a studio was paying for its own tools and central IT had an equal or better option, I made the case with numbers. That is how Perforce and five security products were adopted. Together they saved the studios an estimated $91,000 a year.

**I wrote down how adoption should work.** Every studio asked similar questions: who to contact, how long it takes, what happens to their data. I turned the answers into a Studio Adoption Playbook, so the next adoption would be faster. <!-- TODO(Vincent): confirm what the playbook contained. -->

**I brought European needs into global roadmaps.** As EU Product Owner, I collected requirements from the studios and discussed priorities with the product teams in the US and Asia. <!-- TODO(Vincent): one concrete example of a change you got into a roadmap would be great here. -->

**I helped with the parts between systems and people.** I worked with HR and Finance on onboarding, so a new person at a studio had working accounts and equipment on day one. <!-- TODO(Vincent): confirm. -->

**I coached the people around me.** I coached two junior IT specialists and four interns in daily IT operations.

## What came out of it

Three studios that were polite but distant became regular partners. <!-- TODO(Vincent): is this fair? --> Six products were adopted, with an estimated $91,000 in annual savings, and the playbook meant the next studio did not start from zero.

## What I would do differently

<!-- TODO(Vincent): this reflection is Claude's guess. Replace it with what you really learned. -->

I would measure adoption and satisfaction from the start. The savings were estimated after the fact. With a simple baseline at the beginning, it would have been easier to show the value and to decide what to work on next.

## Why this matters for the work I want to do next

A studio choosing whether to use a central tool behaves a lot like a customer choosing a supplier. It wants to be understood before it is sold to. It wants someone who answers quickly and follows up. And it wants the promises to be kept. That is the role I want to play, with external customers and suppliers.
