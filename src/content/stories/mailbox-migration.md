## The situation

At some point in every company, someone decides to change a tool that everybody uses. For us it was email. Headquarters chose a new system, and every office had to move. <!-- TODO(Vincent): from which system to which? -->

On paper this is an IT task. In reality it is a people task. If mail stops working on a Monday morning, nobody cares how good the new system is. And in Europe the offices were different: different sizes, different languages, different ways of working, and different people who needed to say yes before anything could happen.

I was asked to be the local project manager for Europe. HQ owned the technical side. My job was to make sure Europe was ready on the day.

## The hard part

The technical plan came from HQ, and it was written for HQ. It assumed that everyone read the same announcements, understood the same instructions and could get help in the same time zone. That was not true for us.

There was also no clear picture of who was ready. "Ready" meant many small things: the account was set up, the person knew what would change, their phone was configured, shared mailboxes were moved, and they knew where to go if something broke. Nobody was tracking that per office.

And the deadline was fixed. Other regions depended on the same timeline, so Europe could not simply ask for more time.

## What I did

**I made readiness visible, office by office.** I built a simple tracker with one row per office and one column per readiness item. Every week I went through it with the local contacts. It was not a clever tool, but it meant we always knew where the gaps were, and I could raise a risk early instead of on the last day. <!-- TODO(Vincent): how often did you review it, and who saw it? -->

**I rewrote the instructions for the people who would use them.** The HQ material was correct, but it was long and technical. I wrote two user manuals: one short guide for everyone, and one for the cases that caused the most questions. <!-- TODO(Vincent): what were the two manuals about exactly? --> I kept the language plain and used screenshots for every step.

**I ran Q&A sessions before go-live, not after.** People could ask anything, and I collected the questions. Many questions came up more than once, so I added them to the manuals. <!-- TODO(Vincent): true? --> By go-live day most people had already seen the answer to their question.

**I offered one-on-one help for the people who needed it most.** Some colleagues were nervous about the change, or had special setups. I helped them one by one and moved things over together. <!-- TODO(Vincent): in person, on calls, or both? --> It took time, but those are exactly the people who would otherwise have had a bad first day.

**I agreed on a clear go/no-go moment.** A few days before the switch, we went through the tracker together and decided per office if it was safe to go. <!-- TODO(Vincent): was there a formal go/no-go? If not, remove this paragraph. -->

## What came out of it

Europe went live on the planned date. After the switch, there were zero complaints from users. There were questions, of course, but they were answered quickly because the answers were already written down.

The part I am most proud of came later. Other regions asked for my manuals and used them for their own migration. The documentation outlived the project, which is what good documentation should do.

## What I would do differently

<!-- TODO(Vincent): this reflection is Claude's guess. Replace it with what you really learned. -->

I would start the readiness tracker on day one, not when the plan was already running. The first weeks were spent finding out what "ready" meant, and that time could have gone into preparing people.

I would also ask HQ earlier to review the local material. We worked well together in the end, but a shorter feedback loop at the start would have saved a few rounds of changes.

## Why this matters for the work I want to do next

A customer go-live in a hardware or supplier project looks different, but the core is the same. Somebody has to know exactly who is ready and who is not, raise the risk before it becomes a problem, and write things down so the next person does not start from zero. That is the part of project work I enjoy most.
